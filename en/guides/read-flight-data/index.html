Reading flight data: altitude, heading, speed

Every aircraft shown in Plane GO comes with numbers. Read correctly, they tell you what the aircraft is doing: taking off, cruising, getting ready to land. Here is how to interpret them.

Altitude

Aviation counts in feet (1 foot = 30.48 cm). A few benchmarks:

  • Below 3,000 ft (900 m): aircraft on final approach or just after take-off, or a small light aircraft.
  • 10,000 to 25,000 ft (3,000 to 7,600 m): climbing or descending, regional aircraft in cruise.
  • 30,000 to 41,000 ft (9,000 to 12,500 m): airliner cruise. Long-haul flights often fly at the top of this range.

Above a certain altitude (often around 5,000 ft in France), altitude is given as a “flight level”: FL350 means 35,000 ft.

Speed

The speed shown is the ground speed, in knots (1 knot = 1.852 km/h) or km/h. A cruising airliner moves at 800–950 km/h relative to the ground; wind can add or remove more than 150 km/h. On approach it slows to around 250–300 km/h.

Heading

The heading is the direction the aircraft is moving in, in degrees: 0° = north, 90° = east, 180° = south, 270° = west. Combined with its position, it tells you whether it is coming towards you or moving away. In the Scanner, each aircraft's arrow shows which way to look.

Climb or descent rate

Expressed in feet per minute. An aircraft descending at more than 1,000 ft/min below 10,000 ft is very probably preparing to land. Plane GO uses this to avoid announcing as “visible” an aircraft that will have landed before reaching you.

Guessing the phase of flight

By combining these numbers with the route (departure and arrival airports), you can work out the phase of flight:

  • Far from both airports, high and fast: cruise.
  • Near departure, climbing: take-off and climb.
  • Less than 150 km from arrival, descending: descent and approach.

This is exactly the reasoning needed in the “Which one is flying highest?” challenge of Plane GO's On the map game.

Callsign and registration

The callsign (AFR1234, EZY56MA…) identifies the flight; it changes from one flight to the next. The registration (F-HBXA…) and the hex code identify the aircraft itself. In Plane GO, an aircraft can only be caught once, even if it flies over again under another flight number: its hex code is what counts.
